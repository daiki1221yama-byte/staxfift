## The real pain: outgrowing Facebook Groups and Discord

Free community tools are a great way to start. Plenty of successful memberships began as a Facebook Group or a Discord server. But once people are paying, the cracks start to show:

- **You don't control the experience.** Your members see ads, unrelated notifications, and distractions from the platform itself. Your brand is a guest in someone else's house.
- **Payments live somewhere else.** You charge through one tool, manually add people to the group, and manually remove them when they cancel. Miss one, and someone keeps free access for months.
- **Content gets buried.** Your best posts, recordings, and resources disappear under a feed. New members can't find the "start here" material.
- **Courses and events are scattered.** Recordings in one place, calendar in another, course lessons in a third app. Members have to hunt for value they've already paid for.
- **You can't see what's working.** Without clear data on who's active and who's drifting, you only find out someone was disengaged when they cancel.

A dedicated community platform puts discussions, content, events, and payments under your brand, in one place. The goal isn't fancy features. It's making it easy for members to get value — so they stay.

## How to choose the best community platform for paid memberships: 5 criteria

These are the criteria I'd use to evaluate any community platform. They're based on how memberships succeed or fail, not on any particular feature release.

### 1. Member experience

How easy is it for a new member to log in, find the right conversation, and feel welcome? Look at navigation, notifications, search, and the mobile experience. If your members are mostly on their phones, the app matters more than the desktop dashboard.

### 2. Monetization and fees

Can you sell memberships, subscriptions, and one-time offers directly in the platform? Most platforms combine a subscription fee with transaction fees, and those fees often shrink as you move to higher plans. Compare the total cost at your expected revenue, not just the monthly fee.

### 3. Content beyond discussion

Most paid communities offer more than a chat room: courses, live events, recordings, resource libraries. Check whether these are built in and how well they work together.

### 4. Engagement tools

Gamification, onboarding checklists, member directories, automations, and analytics can help keep members active. None of these replace good community management, but they make it easier for a small team to keep things lively.

### 5. Brand control and growth path

Can you use your own domain? Is there a branded mobile app, and at what plan level? Can you connect your email tool or website? Think about what you'll need when your membership doubles.

### Circle, in plain English

Circle is a community platform built for creators, coaches, and brands that want their members in a space they control. You organize your community into "spaces" — for discussions, events, courses, or resources — and members move between them like rooms in a clubhouse. It's designed to look clean and feel calm, which matters more than people expect: a tidy community is easier to come back to every day.

Based on Circle's official pages, here's what stands out:

- **Unlimited members on every plan.** You pay by plan level and features, not per member. That makes growth easier to plan for.
- **Spaces, courses, and live events in one place.** Discussions, course lessons, live events, and live streams all sit under the same login.
- **Built-in payments.** Branded checkout, paid memberships, and subscriptions, so you don't have to manually add and remove members.
- **Gamification and engagement tools** to encourage participation.
- **Workflows and AI features** on higher tiers, to automate repetitive tasks like welcoming new members or tagging people.
- **Mobile and desktop apps** for members on all plans, with fully custom branded apps available on the top Circle Plus tier.
- **Email Hub** as an add-on, if you want to send newsletters and announcements from the same platform.

Circle offers a 14-day free trial with no credit card required, and its pricing page also mentions a money-back guarantee.

**The minor negative point:** Circle charges transaction fees on every plan, though the rate drops as you move up. If you sell a lot through the platform, factor that into your cost comparison. A fully branded mobile app is limited to the custom-priced top tier, and email marketing is an add-on rather than built in. Some users in public reviews have also mentioned frustration with pricing changes, so it's worth checking the current plan details carefully before you commit.

### Skool

Skool keeps things deliberately simple: a community feed, courses, a calendar, and a points-and-levels system that rewards participation. Many creators like that members don't have to learn much — everything is on a few clear tabs. Pricing is two flat tiers with unlimited members, and the main difference between them is the transaction fee, which is much lower on the higher tier.

The trade-off is flexibility. Skool doesn't try to be your website, email platform, or highly customized community. If you love its format, that's a feature. If you want more control over layout and branding, you may feel boxed in.

### Mighty Networks

Mighty Networks combines community, courses, and events with a strong focus on engagement. Its pricing page lists private groups, activity feeds, chat spaces, on-demand and cohort courses, online and in-person events with livestreaming, paid memberships, and gamification features like points, leaderboards, streaks, and badges. There's also an AI assistant for hosts.

Members are unlimited on every tier, and transaction fees decrease on higher plans. There's a 14-day free trial with no credit card. Custom branded apps are part of the top Mighty Pro offering. Mighty Networks is a strong option if you run cohort-based programs and want lots of engagement tools out of the box.

### Kajabi

Kajabi is an all-in-one platform for knowledge businesses: website, landing pages, email, courses, coaching, and community. The community is one part of a larger system rather than the center of it. Lower plans include one community per brand; the top plan includes more and adds a branded app.

Kajabi says it takes no revenue share, but card processing fees apply, and an extra fee applies if you use a third-party payment processor on lower plans. There's a 14-day free trial. Choose Kajabi if community is one piece of a broader business and you want marketing tools built in.

### Heartbeat

Heartbeat focuses on community operations: channels and discussions, events with RSVPs and reminders, evergreen and cohort courses, paid memberships with checkout pages, and workflow automations. It also includes an AI tool and member badges.

Pricing is flat-tiered, with plans capped by member count, and transaction fees that drop on higher plans. There's a 14-day free trial with no credit card. One detail to know: according to the FAQ, a trial community that isn't upgraded is deactivated and later deleted, so export anything you want to keep. Heartbeat suits community managers who value automations and structured events.

### Discourse

Discourse is a forum-style discussion platform, and it's open source. You can self-host it for free if you have the technical skills, or use the hosted version, which offers a free plan with limits on staff seats, page views, and storage. Paid hosted tiers add more staff seats and features like events, gamification, and analytics.

Discourse is excellent for searchable, long-form discussion — the kind of community where a great answer is still useful a year later. It isn't built around courses or polished membership sales, so creators selling a premium membership experience often prefer Circle or Mighty Networks.

### Thinkific Communities

Thinkific is a course platform first, and communities are attached to that. Plans include a growing number of communities and spaces, with live events and paid memberships available on every tier. There's no free plan, but there's a 30-day free trial.

If your business is mainly courses and you want a community for your students, Thinkific keeps everything in one place. If community is the main product, a community-first platform will usually give members a richer experience.

Many community builders narrow their shortlist to Circle and Skool. Here's how they differ.

**The rule of thumb:** if you want your community to feel like *your* brand, start with Circle. If you want to launch fast in a proven, simple format, start with Skool.

## How to move your members from Facebook or Discord without losing them

Switching platforms is the moment most communities lose people. Here's a simple migration plan.

1. **Build before you announce.** Set up your spaces, welcome post, and "start here" guide first. An empty community feels unwelcoming.
2. **Invite a founding group.** Bring in your 10–20 most active members a week early. Their posts make the new space feel alive when everyone else arrives.
3. **Explain the "why."** Tell members what they gain: easier-to-find content, fewer distractions, better events. Keep it about them, not about you.
4. **Set a clear switch date.** Run both spaces for a short overlap, then close or archive the old one. Two active communities split the conversation.
5. **Host a launch event.** A live welcome call or workshop in the new platform gives everyone a reason to log in on day one.
6. **Check in personally.** In the first two weeks, reach out to members who haven't logged in. A short, friendly message often makes the difference.

## 5 mistakes that quietly kill paid communities

**1. Too many spaces.** Ten channels with three posts each feel empty. Start with a few focused spaces and add more only when conversations demand them.

**2. No onboarding.** New members should know exactly what to do in their first five minutes: introduce themselves, read one key resource, and join the next event.

**3. Relying on the founder for every post.** Encourage member-led threads, peer accountability groups, and member spotlights so the community doesn't depend on you alone.

**4. Ignoring the mobile experience.** Many members check in from their phones. Test the app experience as carefully as the desktop one.

**5. Choosing by monthly price only.** Transaction fees, add-ons, and the tools you still need to connect all affect the real cost. Do the math at your expected revenue.
