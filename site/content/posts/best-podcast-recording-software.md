## The real pain: great conversations, unusable recordings

If you've recorded a remote interview over a regular video call app, you know the problems:

- **Internet hiccups get "baked in."** When audio and video are recorded from the call stream, every lag, glitch, and drop in quality ends up in your final file.
- **Everyone is on one track.** If your guest coughs while you're talking, or someone's mic is much louder, you can't fix one voice without affecting the other.
- **Editing takes forever.** Scrubbing through an hour of waveform to find the five minutes worth cutting can eat an entire evening.
- **Clips and captions are a separate job.** Social media wants short, captioned vertical clips. Making those by hand is a second full-time job.

For a solo creator or a two- or three-person team, these problems decide whether a show survives. The shows that keep going are the ones where recording is reliable and post-production is quick.

## How to judge podcast recording software: 4 criteria that matter

### 1. Recording quality that survives bad internet

The most important feature is **local recording**: each participant's audio and video are recorded on their own device, then uploaded. That way, even if the live connection stutters, the final files stay clean. Also check the maximum audio and video quality and whether you get **separate tracks** for each person.

### 2. Guest experience

Your guest shouldn't need to install anything, create an account, or follow a tech checklist. The best tools work in a browser with a simple link. Bonus points for clear prompts that tell guests to keep the tab open while their files finish uploading.

### 3. Editing speed

Look at how fast you can go from raw recording to a finished episode. **Text-based editing** — where you edit the transcript and the audio/video follows — is a huge time-saver for spoken content. Automatic noise cleanup, level matching, and filler-word removal help too.

### 4. Repurposing: clips, captions, and transcripts

A modern podcast lives on several platforms. Check whether the tool can generate short clips, add captions, and export transcripts, and how much of that is automatic.

And as always, consider how the **pricing model** works: is it based on recording hours, number of seats, features, or all three?

### Riverside, in plain English

Riverside was built specifically for recording remote conversations at studio quality. Each participant is recorded locally on their own device — up to high-resolution video and uncompressed-style audio, depending on your plan — and the files upload in the background during and after the session. Because of that, a shaky Wi-Fi connection affects the live conversation, not the final recording.

Highlights from the documentation and user feedback:

- **Separate audio and video tracks for every participant**, so you can fix one person's levels without touching anyone else.
- **Browser-based guest experience.** Guests join from a link; no account required for them.
- **Text-based editor.** Edit the transcript to cut sections, and the audio and video follow along.
- **Automatic transcription**, which you can export for show notes, blog posts, or accessibility.
- **AI clip creation ("Magic Clips")** that suggests short, captioned moments for social media.
- **Live streaming options** if you want to broadcast while recording.

**The minor negative point:** Riverside's plans are built around recording-hour limits and feature tiers, so if you record a lot — say, several long episodes a week — you'll want to check that your plan's hours fit your schedule. Also, because files upload from each guest's device, guests need to keep the tab open until uploading finishes. Most people are fine with this, but it's worth a polite reminder at the end of each session.

### Descript

Descript made text-based editing popular, and it's still the tool many editors love most. You edit your episode like a document: delete a sentence from the transcript, and it disappears from the audio and video. Features like automatic filler-word removal and studio-style voice enhancement can make a home recording sound much more polished.

Descript also includes remote recording, so you can record and edit in one app. Many creators choose based on which side matters more: **Riverside leans toward recording quality and the guest experience; Descript leans toward deep editing.** Some even record in Riverside and edit in Descript.

### Zencastr

Zencastr is one of the early names in remote podcast recording and has an audio-first heritage. It's straightforward, works in the browser, and has a free plan, which makes it a friendly starting point for audio-only shows. Its editing and video features are lighter than Riverside's or Descript's, so it's best for creators who want simple, reliable recording and do their editing elsewhere.

### StreamYard

StreamYard is primarily a browser-based live streaming studio. It's excellent at going live to several platforms at once, adding branded overlays, and showing comments on screen. If your show is a live broadcast first and a podcast second, it's a strong pick.

For pure podcast production, though, its focus is different. Local high-quality recordings and separate tracks are tied to paid plans, and you'll typically edit elsewhere.

### Podcastle

Podcastle bundles recording, editing, and a set of AI audio tools — noise removal, voice enhancement, and even AI voices — into one platform at an approachable price. It's a good fit for beginners who want an all-in-one setup without paying for a more premium tool. Video features and overall polish are improving, but creators focused on high-end video podcasts often still lean toward Riverside.

## 5 recording mistakes that no software can fully fix

Even the best tool can't rescue everything. These habits make a bigger difference than most features.

**1. No headphones.** When a guest listens through speakers, your voice leaks back into their mic and creates echo. Ask every guest to wear headphones — even basic earbuds help.

**2. Recording in an echoey room.** Bare walls and hard floors make voices sound distant. A room with a rug, curtains, or a bookshelf sounds far better than a fancy microphone in a kitchen.

**3. Laptop mics on both ends.** A simple USB microphone is one of the best upgrades you can make. If a guest doesn't have one, at least ask them to sit close to their laptop and avoid typing.

**4. Skipping a quick sound check.** Spend two minutes at the start listening back to a short test. It's much easier to fix a muted mic or wrong input before you begin than to explain it to a guest afterward.

**5. Ending the session too fast.** With tools that upload locally recorded files, closing the tab too early can mean missing footage. Build a habit: chat for a minute after you stop recording and let uploads finish.

## A simple recording-to-publishing workflow

If you're a solo creator, here's a lightweight weekly routine that works well with Riverside or any similar tool:

1. **Before the call:** send your guest the link, a reminder to use headphones, and a note that they'll need to keep the tab open at the end.
2. **During the call:** record, keep an eye on the upload indicators, and jot down timestamps of great moments.
3. **Right after:** let uploads finish, then generate the transcript.
4. **Edit by text:** remove false starts, long tangents, and off-topic sections by deleting them from the transcript.
5. **Clean up audio:** apply noise reduction and level matching.
6. **Create clips:** use AI clip suggestions as a starting point, then pick the three strongest moments and check the captions.
7. **Publish and repurpose:** export the episode, use the transcript for show notes and a blog post, and schedule your clips.

Following this every week turns podcasting from an overwhelming project into a predictable routine.

## How to test a recorder before committing

During a free plan or trial, run one realistic test rather than clicking through menus:

- Record a 20-minute conversation with a friend in another location — ideally someone with average internet.
- Ask them to join from a laptop browser without any special setup.
- After the call, check: Did both tracks upload? How do the files sound and look? How long did it take to make a 3-minute highlight with captions?

That one test tells you more than any feature list.

## Making remote video interviews look good

If you publish video episodes, a few simple choices make remote interviews look far more professional — no expensive gear required.

**Light your face from the front.** Sit facing a window or place a simple lamp behind your camera. Light from behind you turns you into a silhouette.

**Raise the camera to eye level.** Stack a few books under your laptop. Looking slightly up at the camera is rarely flattering, and eye level feels more natural to viewers.

**Tidy the background.** It doesn't need to be a studio. A bookshelf, a plant, or a plain wall works well. Just remove clutter and anything you wouldn't want on the internet.

**Frame consistently.** Ask guests to sit so their head and shoulders fill most of the frame. When you place two people side by side in editing, similar framing looks much more polished.

**Send a short guest guide.** A friendly one-page note with these tips — plus "use headphones" and "keep the tab open at the end" — makes guests feel prepared and dramatically improves your raw footage.

## Riverside vs Descript: the comparison most creators ask about

Riverside and Descript come up together so often that they deserve a closer look.

**Where each one started.** Riverside started as a recording tool and added editing. Descript started as an editing tool and added recording. That history still shows: each is strongest in the area where it began.

**Recording.** Both record locally and give you separate tracks. Riverside puts a lot of attention on the live session itself — the guest's experience in the browser, upload progress indicators, and high-resolution video. If you regularly interview guests who aren't technical, that focus is reassuring.

**Editing.** Both offer text-based editing. Descript's editor is generally considered deeper, with more control over scenes, layouts, and audio effects. Riverside's editor is designed to be quick and approachable, which many solo creators prefer.

**Clips and repurposing.** Both can help you create short clips with captions. Riverside's AI clip suggestions are designed to pull highlights straight out of your recordings, which is handy when you publish on several social platforms every week.

**The simple rule of thumb:** if your biggest worry is "will this recording come out clean?", start with Riverside. If your biggest worry is "how do I edit faster?", start with Descript. If both matter equally, many creators record in Riverside and edit in Descript.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do my guests need to download anything?**
With Riverside and most modern tools, guests join through a browser link on a laptop or desktop. Mobile options exist too, but a laptop with headphones usually gives the best results.

**Is a free plan good enough to start a podcast?**
For testing your setup and recording a few episodes, often yes. Free plans usually limit recording time, export quality, or add a watermark, so most regular shows upgrade once they publish consistently.

**Do I still need a separate editing app?**
Not necessarily. Riverside, Descript, and Podcastle all include editors that cover most interview shows. Creators who want very detailed sound design or complex video edits sometimes still export to a dedicated editing app.

**What's the single best upgrade for sound quality?**
A decent USB microphone and headphones — for you and, ideally, your guest — usually make a bigger difference than any software setting.

**Can I use the transcript for show notes or a blog post?**
Yes, and it's one of the easiest ways to get more value from every episode. Clean up the transcript a little, add headings, and you have the base for show notes or an article.
